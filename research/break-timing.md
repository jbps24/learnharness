# Research: when and how to suggest breaks during a lesson

Wayfinder ticket: break suggestions in teaching lessons.

Questions:

1. Is about 45 minutes a supported interval between breaks, compared with Pomodoro's 25, an
   ultradian 90, or other intervals? How strong is that evidence?
2. Does a break placed at a topic or event boundary (a closed block of material, such as a finished
   chapter) help retention or attention more than a break at a fixed time?
3. How long should a short break be, and what kind of break works (rest, movement, no screen, eyes
   closed)?
4. Does a gap that happens anyway (the learner wanders off for some minutes) count as a break for
   these purposes?

Scope: published learning-science, cognitive-psychology, HCI and ergonomics literature, read in
November 2026. Primary papers and meta-analyses were used where they could be reached. Where only
an abstract or a citing paper was read, the entry says so. This note reports evidence. It does not
choose a design.

How to read the ratings:

- **Strong**: several independent studies or a meta-analysis with consistent results, applicable to
  adult self-study.
- **Moderate**: a meta-analysis with small or mixed effects, or several studies that point the same
  way but in a different setting.
- **Weak**: one or two small studies, indirect evidence, or practice guidance with no data behind it.
- **None**: no study found that tests the claim.

## 1. Is ~45 minutes a supported interval?

### What was found

**No primary study compares 45 minutes with other study-block lengths.** Searches for 45- and 50-minute
blocks found study-skills pages that recommend them but cite no data. The intervals that have been tested
directly are 12, 24 and 25 minutes (below). The 90-minute figure comes from a hypothesis about sleep
cycles.

**Pomodoro and systematic breaks, tested on students' own self-study:**

- Biwer, Wiradhany, oude Egbrink & de Bruin (2023), *Br J Educ Psychol* 93(S2):353-367,
  [doi:10.1111/bjep.12593](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/). Online study of students' own
  self-study sessions. Three conditions: self-regulated breaks (n = 35), 24 min study + 6 min break
  ("Pomodoro", n = 25), and 12 min + 3 min (n = 27). With self-regulated breaks, students studied and
  rested for longer, and reported more fatigue and distraction and less concentration and motivation.
  The systematic conditions finished a similar amount of work in less time. *Quality:* small samples, a
  single session, self-report outcomes, and no objective measure of learning.
- Smits, Wenzel & de Bruin (2025), *Behavioral Sciences* 15(7):861,
  [doi:10.3390/bs15070861](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/). A 2-hour self-study
  session, randomized, N = 94 analyzed: self-regulated (25), Pomodoro 25/5 (36), and Flowtime (33;
  break length scaled to the preceding block: 5 min after ≤25 min of study, 8 min after 25–50, 10 min
  after >50). **There were no overall group differences** in motivation, productivity, fatigue, task
  completion or flow. In Pomodoro, fatigue rose faster and motivation fell faster over the session than
  with self-regulated breaks. The authors recommend no single technique. *Quality:* small, 93% female,
  one program, single-item self-report scales, no learning measure, and compliance not monitored.
  It partly contradicts Biwer et al. 2023.

**The 90-minute ultradian cycle (Kleitman's "basic rest-activity cycle"):** Kleitman proposed in 1961
that the roughly 90-minute NREM-REM sleep cycle continues through waking hours. Some EEG and performance
studies find rhythms of about 90 to 100 minutes in waking alertness. Others find none, for example
Kripke's work from 1977 on and Lack's reanalysis of sleep-deprivation data. Reviews conclude that no
common waking cycle locked to the sleep cycle has been confirmed. (Sources: the
[PubMed abstract of a 1994 ultradian EEG/performance study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7870505/)
and chapter summaries found through search. The original papers were not read.) *Quality:* weak and
contested as a basis for timing work.

**Vigilance research on time-on-task:**

- Performance on monotonous monitoring tasks falls within a session. A meta-analysis of 42 experiments
  found the drop in sensitivity is substantial and depends on the type of discrimination and the event
  rate (See, Howe, Warm & Dember 1995, *Psychol Bull* 117:230-249,
  [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.117.2.230](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.117.2.230); abstract and citing
  papers only).
- Ariga & Lleras (2011), *Cognition* 118:439-443: two brief task switches during a vigilance task
  prevented the decline (N = 84, according to the university press release).
  [Abstract](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/brief-and-rare-mental-breaks-keep-you-focused-deactivation-and-re/).
- Helton & Russell (2012, *Exp Brain Res*) did **not** replicate this: every group still declined.
  Helton & Russell (2015), "Rest is best", *Cognition* 134:165-173,
  [doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2014.10.001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25460389/), found that a full
  rest break restored vigilance best, and continuous work worst.
- Vigilance tasks are not studying, so these results transfer only loosely.

**Lecture "attention span":** Bradbury (2016), *Adv Physiol Educ* 40:509-513
([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)), is an opinion piece. It traces the widely
repeated 10–15-minute attention span back to thin evidence. Risko et al. (2012, *Appl Cogn Psychol*
26:234-242) found that mind wandering increased and retention fell over a recorded video lecture. Live
lectures did not show the same rise (as summarized by later reviews; the original was not read).

**Ergonomics and workplace guidance:**

- UK HSE guidance for screen work ([hse.gov.uk](https://www.hse.gov.uk/msd/dse/work-routine.htm)) says
  there is no legal rule on break length or frequency. It recommends short, frequent breaks: "5 to 10
  minutes every hour is better than 20 minutes every 2 hours." It says that ideally users should choose
  when to take breaks. This is guidance, not a study.
- Galinsky et al. (2007), *Am J Ind Med* 50:519-527
  ([NIOSH record](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/186286)), was a field study of data-entry operators
  (reported N is 51 in one summary and up to 90 volunteers in another). Adding four 5-minute breaks to
  two 15-minute breaks reduced discomfort and eyestrain. Output stayed the same, because keying speed
  rose enough to make up the 20 minutes of lost work time. Its 2000 predecessor (N = 42, *Ergonomics*
  43:622-638) found the same pattern. Because the four extra breaks were spread over the day, this
  supports "a short break about every hour".

### Verdict

- **45 minutes: no direct evidence.** It is within the 25–60-minute range in which regular short breaks
  have been tested and are harmless or helpful.
- Nothing shows 45 beats 25 or 60.
- The 90-minute ultradian rationale is weak.
- The strongest finding is that breaks help fatigue and mood more reliably than they help performance
  (see Albulescu et al. in Q3).
- Fixed schedules and self-chosen breaks perform similarly once a session is long enough (Smits et al.
  2025). One small study found that self-chosen breaks led to longer, more tiring sessions (Biwer et al.
  2023).

## 2. Break at a topic or event boundary vs at a fixed time

### Mechanism: event segmentation (well established in the lab)

- **Boundaries are where the brain stores the event that just ended.**
  - Event Segmentation Theory holds that people keep a working model of the current event and update
    it at boundaries (Zacks et al. 2007; reviewed by Radvansky & Zacks).
  - Swallow, Zacks & Abrams (2009), *J Exp Psychol Gen* 138:236-257
    ([PMC2819197](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2819197)): objects seen at event boundaries
    are remembered better long-term. Information from the previous event becomes harder to retrieve
    once a boundary has passed.
  - Ben-Yakov & Henson (2018), *J Neurosci* 38:10057-10068
    ([PMC6246887](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6246887)), fMRI, n = 253 (8.5-min film) and
    n = 15 (120-min film): the hippocampus responds at boundaries that independent observers agree on,
    and responds more strongly the more salient the boundary. A 2021 erratum did not change the
    conclusions.
  - Silva, Baldassano & Fuentemilla (2019), *J Neurosci*, EEG, n = 30, 50-min film: at event
    boundaries the brain rapidly reactivates the event just seen, and this predicted later recall
    ([preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/511782v1)).
- **More boundaries can improve memory for content split across them.** Pettijohn, Thompson, Tamplin,
  Krawietz & Radvansky (2016), *Cognition* 148:136-144
  ([PubMed 26780472](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26780472/)), ran four experiments using
  doorways, computer windows and narrative shifts. Items split across two events were remembered
  better than items in one event, and adding a second boundary helped more.
  *Caveat:* the related "doorway forgetting" effect has had failed replications (2021, St Andrews).
- **Boundaries also cut associations.** Ezzyat & Davachi (2011, *Psychol Sci*) and DuBrow & Davachi
  (2013, *J Exp Psychol Gen*): memory for links between items, and for their order, is worse when the
  items fall on different sides of a boundary than when they fall in the same event (from abstracts and
  citing reviews).
  - **Implication:** a break inserted in the middle of a unit that should be learned as a whole risks
    splitting it in memory.

### Direct tests of where a break is placed

- **Boltz (1992)**, *J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform* 18:90-105 (full text read,
  [PDF](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Boltz-JEP_HPP92.pdf)). This is the closest direct test.
  - Design: N = 100 (10 per cell). Two 40–45-minute TV drama episodes, with commercial breaks of
    2–2.5 minutes. There were 3 or 6 breaks, placed either at major episode boundaries (chosen by a
    separate rating panel) or inside episodes. Two control groups saw no breaks at all.
  - Recall: breaks at boundaries beat breaks inside episodes (F(1,72) = 95.1, p < .001). **Breaks at
    boundaries also beat no breaks, and no breaks beat breaks inside episodes.**
  - Recognition, temporal order and memory for plot details followed the same pattern. More breaks at
    boundaries increased the benefit.
  - *Quality:* a single study with an immediate test only, using narrative film rather than
    instructional material, and small cells.
  - Later work (Gold, Zacks & Flores 2017, as summarized by Peterson, Rogers & Bailey 2021) found that
    cueing non-boundaries also helped, but less than cueing boundaries. That cue was a signal, not a
    pause. That study was not read here.
- **HCI interruption timing.** This work measures how disruptive an interruption is, not how well
  material is learned.
  - Adamczyk & Bailey (2004), CHI ([PDF](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p271.pdf),
    full text read). Within-subjects, n = 16, editing, search and media tasks. Interruptions at coarse
    subtask boundaries caused less annoyance (56% less than at the worst moment), less frustration and
    less time pressure. **Resumption lag did not differ significantly.**
  - Iqbal & Bailey (2008), CHI ([doi:10.1145/1357054.1357070](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357070),
    abstract only): delaying notifications until a breakpoint reduced frustration and reaction time
    compared with delivering them immediately.

### Related levers (not breaks as such)

- **Spacing.** Distributing study of the same material over time improves retention (Cepeda et al.
  2006, *Psychol Bull* 132:354-380, meta-analysis of 317 experiments,
  [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)).
  - A single break does not create spacing unless the material is revisited after the break.
  - So spacing argues for *returning to* the earlier unit after a break, not for where to put the
    break.
- **Retrieval at segment boundaries.** Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013), *PNAS* 110:6313-6317
  ([PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)), ran two experiments (Exp 2
  n = 48) with a single video lecture. Short tests between lecture segments roughly halved mind
  wandering (about 40% to 20% of probes) and improved learning. A 2016 follow-up (Jing, Szpunar &
  Schacter) found weaker effects on mind wandering.

### Wakeful rest after learning (consolidation)

The idea is that a few minutes of quiet rest straight after learning help consolidate what was just
learned.

- Dewar, Alber, Butler, Cowan & Della Sala (2012), *Psychol Sci* 23:955-960: after hearing a story,
  10 minutes of eyes-closed rest instead of a spot-the-difference game improved recall at 15–30
  minutes and at 7 days.
- Humiston, Tucker, Summer & Wamsley (2019), *Sci Rep*
  ([PMC6920145](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6920145/)), was a preregistered replication
  (n = 31 analyzed) and **found no effect** (d = 0.09). Its meta-analysis of about 11 studies gave
  d = 0.38. More daydreaming during the rest went with *less* benefit (r = −.51).
- Parra, Zhang & Radvansky (2026), *Psychon Bull Rev* 33:49
  ([PMC12808189](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12808189/)), is the largest meta-analysis:
  51 studies and 142 effect sizes.
  - Overall g = 0.34, with high heterogeneity (I² = 63%).
  - **Healthy young and middle-aged adults: g = 0.20, or 0.15 after removing outliers.** Older adults:
    g = 0.57. Amnesic patients: g = 1.18.
  - In adults, effects were larger for free recall than recognition, larger for auditory than read
    material (g 0.38 vs 0.13), and have shrunk over publication years.
  - Rest periods ran from 2 to 60 minutes, with a mean of about 10.5.
  - The authors note that young participants often did not actually rest (they asked to use phones or
    do homework).

What this means for break placement:

- Consolidation-friendly rest is most useful right *after* a unit has been encoded, which is a
  boundary.
- The effect in young adults is small and inconsistent.
- It depends on the break being free of new material and of absorbing activity.

### Verdict

- **Moderate** that boundaries are privileged moments for memory. The lab and neuroimaging literature
  is large and consistent.
- **Weak** direct evidence that a *break* at a boundary beats a break at a fixed time. The best test
  is one 1992 film study (N = 100). Its secondary finding matters most for design: a break in the
  middle of a unit did worse than no break.
- **Weak to moderate, and small in size,** for a consolidation benefit from quiet rest right after a
  unit.
- No study was found that compares boundary-timed and clock-timed breaks for instructional material
  or tutoring.

## 3. How long, and what kind of break?

### Length

- Albulescu et al. (2022), *PLOS ONE* 17(8):e0272460
  ([PMC9432722](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)), meta-analysis of micro-breaks
  (breaks of 10 minutes or less): 22 samples, N = 2,335, breaks from 8 seconds to 10 minutes.
  - Vigor: d = 0.36 (k = 9). Fatigue: d = 0.35 (k = 9).
  - **Performance overall: d = 0.16, not significant** (k = 15, I² = 59%).
  - Performance on *cognitively demanding* tasks: d = −0.09, not significant. Clerical tasks: d = 0.56
    (2 studies). Creative tasks: d = 0.38 (3 studies).
  - Meta-regression: **longer breaks gave larger performance gains** (b = 0.07, p = .006). The authors
    suggest that highly depleting tasks "may need more than 10-minute breaks".
  - Only 4 of 22 studies had low risk of bias. Activity type could not be tested as a moderator. For
    well-being, the authors conclude that "any type of decoupling activity" works.
- Tested schedules: 5 minutes per 25 (Pomodoro); 6 per 24 and 3 per 12 (Biwer et al.); 5–10 minutes
  per hour (HSE); 5 minutes per hour on top of standard breaks (Galinsky et al.); about 10 minutes of
  rest in the wakeful-rest literature.
- An older ergonomics claim (Bills 1943, cited by Steinborn & Huestegge 2016) that breaks should be
  3–6 minutes and not exceed 8 minutes is historical opinion.

### Kind

- **Quiet rest vs a task.** Helton & Russell (2015): full rest restored vigilance better than switching
  to another task. The wakeful-rest literature uses eyes-closed, undisturbed rest. Any distractor task
  removes the benefit (Parra et al. 2026: distractor-absent g = 0.26 vs distractor-present 0.07 in
  adults).
- **Rich mental activity cuts the memory benefit.** Craig, Della Sala & Dewar (2014), *PLoS ONE*
  ([PMC3988030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3988030)): cued autobiographical thinking
  (recalling the past and imagining the future) during the delay reduced recall as much as a visual
  search task. Varma et al. (2018) found the same pattern.
- **Walking vs passive rest.** Steinborn & Huestegge (2016), *Appl Cogn Psychol* 30:795-805
  (full text read): N = 67 (35 continuous work, 32 with breaks). Two 3-minute breaks during about 50
  minutes of mental arithmetic. Breaks helped, more so later in the session and on harder problems.
  **An outdoor walk and watching a video helped equally.**
- **Exercise breaks during a lecture.** Fenesi, Lucibello, Kim & Heisz (2018), *J Appl Res Mem Cogn*
  7:261-269 (abstract and press release; about 77 students enrolled). Three 5-minute calisthenics
  breaks during a 50-minute video lecture beat both no breaks and video-game breaks, for attention and
  for learning at 48 hours. Single lab study.
- **Phone breaks restore less.** Kang & Kurtzberg (2019), *J Behav Addict* 8:395-403
  ([PMC7044622](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044622)): N = 414 solving anagrams.
  Participants who took a break on a phone solved 22% fewer problems afterwards than those in the
  other break conditions combined, and took 19% longer. The phone group did not differ significantly
  from the no-break group. The same break task on a computer screen or on paper restored more, so the
  issue seems to be the phone rather than screens in general. Single study.
- **Nature.** Stevenson, Schilhab & Bentsen (2018), *J Toxicol Environ Health B* 21:227-268
  ([PubMed 30130463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30130463/)), meta-analysis of 42 studies: being in
  or viewing nature gave small-to-moderate improvements in working memory and cognitive flexibility,
  and less reliable improvements in attentional control.
- **Eyes and screens.** Talens-Estarelles et al. (2023), *Cont Lens Anterior Eye* 46:101744
  ([PubMed 35963776](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35963776/)): 29 symptomatic computer users,
  before-and-after design with no control group. Reminders for the 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes,
  look 20 feet away for 20 seconds) reduced eye-strain and dry-eye symptoms over two weeks. The
  improvement was gone one week after the reminders stopped, and clinical signs did not change. This
  addresses eye comfort, not learning.

### Verdict

- **Length: about 5–10 minutes** is the tested range. Within it, longer is better for performance.
  Evidence is moderate for fatigue and vigor and weak for performance.
- **Kind:** for fatigue, almost anything that takes the learner off the task works. For memory, quiet,
  undisturbed rest without new input is the condition that has been tested (eyes closed in most
  studies). Phone use appears to restore less.
- Movement is at least as good as passive rest. One study found it better for lecture learning.
- Each comparison between break types rests on one or two studies, so evidence is **weak**.

## 4. Does a gap that happens anyway count as a break?

No study tests this directly. The inferences below come from adjacent findings.

- **For fatigue and vigor: probably yes**, if the learner actually disengaged. Albulescu et al. found
  well-being benefits from "any type of decoupling activity".
- **For the memory benefit of rest: probably not, if the gap was filled.**
  - The rest benefit disappears with an interfering task (Parra et al. 2026), with cued
    autobiographical thinking (Craig et al. 2014), and is smaller with more daydreaming (Humiston et
    al. 2019).
  - Phone breaks restored less than paper or computer breaks (Kang & Kurtzberg 2019).
  - Wandering off usually means phone use, conversation or other content, and so falls into the
    "interference" conditions.
- **Placement matters for an unplanned gap too.** A gap in the middle of a unit is, in effect, a
  break at a nonbreakpoint, which did worse than no break in Boltz (1992). Cross-boundary association
  and order memory suffer (Ezzyat & Davachi 2011; DuBrow & Davachi 2013).
- **Resuming after a gap has a cost.**
  - Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis (2008), *J Exp Psychol Appl* 14:299-313 (abstract read): resumption
    time grows with the length of the interruption and with how demanding it is. This held for
    interruptions up to 1 minute, which is consistent with decay of the suspended goal.
  - Katidioti, Borst, van Vugt & Taatgen (2016), *Comput Hum Behav* 63:906-915: self-interruptions
    were *more* disruptive overall than external interruptions at the same moments. People chose to
    self-interrupt at low-workload moments. Resumption lag was the same; the extra cost came from
    the roughly 1-second decision to interrupt.
  - Both studies measure seconds to a minute in lab tasks. Nothing found measures a multi-minute gap
    in studying.
- **Self-timed breaks vs scheduled breaks.**
  - Biwer et al. (2023): self-regulated breaks were longer and came with more fatigue.
  - Smits et al. (2025): over 2 hours, self-regulated breaks were no worse.
  - HSE guidance prefers that users choose their own breaks.
  - So it is unclear whether an unplanned gap should reset the clock for the next suggested break.
    The evidence does not settle it.

### Verdict

**Weak.** An unplanned gap plausibly counts toward fatigue relief. It plausibly does not count as
consolidation-friendly rest unless the learner was idle and away from screens. A gap in the middle of
a unit may hurt memory for that unit, so a short recap on return is the evidence-adjacent remedy (the
retrieval effects from Q2). These are inferences, not tested findings.

## Gaps and caveats

- No study found tests break timing in one-to-one or AI tutoring, or with adult self-paced learners on
  instructional (non-narrative) material, against an objective learning outcome.
- Most break studies use self-report outcomes (fatigue, motivation) and single sessions.
- Wakeful-rest effects in young adults are small (g ≈ 0.15–0.20) and have shrunk over publication
  years.
- Several sources were read only as abstracts or through citing papers. These are marked above: See
  et al. 1995, Ariga & Lleras 2011, Ezzyat & Davachi 2011, DuBrow & Davachi 2013, Dewar et al. 2012,
  Fenesi et al. 2018, Iqbal & Bailey 2008, Gold et al. 2017, Risko et al. 2012, and the ultradian
  literature.
- Full texts read: Boltz 1992, Adamczyk & Bailey 2004, Steinborn & Huestegge 2016, Monk et al. 2008
  (abstract and method), Humiston et al. 2019, Albulescu et al. 2022, Parra et al. 2026, and Smits
  et al. 2025.
